# Hidden History: 30 Stories of Islam’s Connections with the West

*A reader-friendly overview of Joram van Klaveren’s* *Vergeten Verleden* 

> **Editorial note:** This is an independent, topic-level overview based on the story titles and general historical context—not a substitute for the full chapters. Some titles make bold historical claims. Those claims should be checked against the book’s evidence and reputable specialist sources before being repeated as established fact.

## Introduction

Islamic history and Western history have never been entirely separate. Across centuries, people, ideas, technologies, trade routes, wars, migrations, and religious encounters have connected Muslim societies with Europe and the Americas. In *Hidden History*, Joram van Klaveren uses 30 short stories to draw attention to connections that may be unfamiliar to many readers.

The stories range from the Dutch revolt and Ottoman diplomacy to the legacy of al-Andalus, the intellectual work of Ibn Khaldun, and the life of boxer Muhammad Ali. Some concern well-documented historical figures and exchanges; others advance provocative interpretations that deserve closer examination.

    <h3>1. The Founding of the Netherlands: Islamic DNA</h3>
    <p>Van Klaveren argues that Islamic history is woven into the origins of the Netherlands, despite modern political claims that the country’s heritage is exclusively Jewish-Christian.</p>

<p>He explains that William of Orange, a central figure in the Dutch revolt against Spanish rule, sought support from the Ottoman Empire. Spain’s King Philip II was determined to suppress Protstantism in his territories, and the Dutch resistance needed powerful allies against the Spanish military. According to van Klaveren, the Ottomans helped by intensifying their military campaigns against Spain in the Mediterranean, diverting Spanish resources and helping the Dutch cause.</p>

<p>The interview also highlights the expression associated with the liberation of Leiden in 1574: “Rather Turkish than papist.” Williams and van Klaveren discuss how this reflected the willingness of some Dutch Protestants to identify with the Ottoman Muslims rather than the Catholic Spanish authorities persecuting them.</p>

<p>For van Klaveren, this history challenges the notion that Islam has always been alien to the Netherlands. He argues that Ottoman support helped shape the country's emergence as an independent state.</p>

<p>Central message: The history of the Netherlands cannot be fully understood without acknowledging its historical connections with the Muslim world.</p>

    <h3>2. Islam: One of America’s Pillars</h3>
<p>
  In this chapter, van Klaveren points to the artwork The Evolution of Civilization, painted by Edwin Blashfield for the dome of the Library of Congress in Washington, DC. The work depicts different civilisations and intellectual traditions that contributed to human progress, including Islam.</p><p>
  
  Williams and van Klaveren emphasise the significance of Islam's inclusion among these traditions. They discuss how the painting associates Islamic civilisation with lawgiving, challenging the modern portrayal of Islam as inherently opposed to intellectual development, scientific progress or modernity.</p><p>
  
  The story is particularly striking because the United States is frequently presented as a defining example of Western civilisation. Van Klaveren uses the artwork to argue that Islamic contributions have been acknowledged within major Western cultural institutions, even if they are less familiar in contemporary political discussions.</p><p>
  
  Central message: Islamic civilisation has been recognised as a contributor to the intellectual and cultural foundations of the West, including in the United States.

    <h3>3. A Crusader Becomes Muslim</h3>
    <p>
      Van Klaveren recounts the story of Robert of St Albans, an English crusader who, according to the historical accounts he discusses, converted to Islam during the Crusades.</p><p>
      
      Born around 1160, Robert was described in the interview as a Templar knight who fought to secure Christian access to the Holy Land. During a battle, he was wounded and captured by Muslim forces. Rather than being killed, he was treated while he recovered.</p><p>
      
      During his captivity, Robert heard the Islamic call to prayer and became interested in the religion. He reportedly asked questions, encountered Saladin, the Muslim ruler celebrated for his role in the Crusades, and eventually embraced Islam. Van Klaveren further recounts that Robert married Saladin’s niece and subsequently fought alongside Muslim forces against the crusaders.</p><p>
      
      According to the account presented in the interview, Robert eventually died fighting against his former comrades. Van Klaveren regards this as a remarkable example of an individual crossing the religious and political boundaries that are often assumed to have defined medieval Europe.</p><p>
      
      Central message: Religious identity in the medieval period was not always fixed by birthplace or upbringing; even a prominent crusader could embrace the faith of those he had once fought.</p>
      <blockquote>
      <p>
      
      Editorial note: Van Klaveren presents Robert as the earliest known English convert to Islam, while acknowledging that earlier converts may have existed. The historical details and identification would benefit from checking against the sources cited in the book.
    </p></blockquote>

    <h3>4. An Ottoman Sultan Saves the Irish Population</h3>
    <p>
      This chapter examines the Ottoman Empire’s reported assistance to Ireland during the Great Famine of 1847, when widespread hunger devastated the country. Van Klaveren recalls that approximately one million people died and another million emigrated during the famine.</p><p>
      
      According to his account, Ottoman Sultan Abdulmejid I was moved by the suffering of the Irish population and by the Islamic teaching that a believer should not remain satisfied while a neighbour goes hungry. He decided to provide financial assistance and send food and medical supplies.</p><p>
      
      Van Klaveren says that Queen Victoria objected to the Sultan giving more money than the British contribution, prompting him to reduce the publicly stated amount. He then recounts that Ottoman ships secretly carried provisions to Ireland, ultimately reaching Drogheda after their original route to Dublin was reportedly obstructed.</p><p>
      
      The interview connects this episode to the Irish town’s continuing historical associations with Turkey. Williams and van Klaveren mention Drogheda United football club, whose nickname is “the Turks” and whose badge features a crescent and star.</p><p>
      
      For van Klaveren, the story illustrates an act of solidarity motivated by religious conviction at a time when Ireland was experiencing devastating hardship.</p><p>
      
      Central message: Islamic charitable principles inspired an act of humanitarian assistance to a predominantly Christian population in one of Ireland’s darkest periods.</p>
      <blockquote><p>
      
      Editorial note: The Sultan’s assistance and the details of the alleged British obstruction are historical claims that should be checked against archival and scholarly sources before being presented as settled fact.

    <h3>6. Dracula Stopped by Muslims</h3>
<p>
  This chapter explores the historical figure behind the Dracula legend, Vlad, and his relationship with the Ottoman Empire.</p><p>
  
  The author explains that Vlad and his brother Radu were sent to be raised in the Ottoman Empire as part of an arrangement between their father and the Sultan. Radu adapted to his surroundings and converted to Islam, while Vlad became hostile towards the Ottomans.</p><p>
  
  Vlad later refused to pay the tribute owed to the Ottoman Empire and committed brutal acts against Ottoman representatives and others. The author recounts stories of Vlad's extreme cruelty, including the execution of prisoners by impalement.</p><p>
  
  He says that Vlad was eventually defeated and killed in 1476, with Radu and Mehmed II playing roles in bringing his rule to an end.</p><p>
  
  The chapter's central argument is that Muslim forces helped end the tyranny of one of Europe's most brutal rulers, offering a historical perspective that contrasts with popular portrayals of Dracula.</p><p>

## Themes that connect the stories

### 1. Diplomacy and conflict

Several stories place Muslim powers and individuals within European political struggles—from Ottoman-Dutch contacts to the conflicts surrounding Vlad the Impaler. They show that alliances often crossed religious boundaries when political interests overlapped.

### 2. Knowledge, law, and ideas

The chapters on Goethe, Ibn Khaldun, and Napoleon raise questions about intellectual exchange. A careful historical account distinguishes documented influence from parallel ideas or later speculation.

### 3. Everyday culture and built environments

Stories about irrigation, Malta, Portugal, architecture, and food explore how cultural practices can endure and change over generations. These subjects are especially useful for examining the difference between a broad cultural legacy and a specific claim about origins.

### 4. People whose lives crossed boundaries

Jan Janszoon/Murat Reis, Mustafa Azemmouri/Estevanico, William Henry Quilliam, and Muhammad Ali illustrate very different forms of movement, conversion, and public identity.

### 5. Reading provocative history critically

An arresting title is an invitation to investigate, not proof by itself. To assess each claim, look for contemporary documents, specialist scholarship, clear definitions, and alternative interpretations. Be particularly careful with claims that a familiar European institution, symbol, food, or law has a single Islamic origin.

## Conclusion

*Hidden History* invites readers to look beyond a simple story in which Islam and the West developed in isolation. Its 30 topics offer starting points for exploring diplomacy, migration, scholarship, art, religion, and everyday life across cultural boundaries. The most rewarding way to read the book is with curiosity—and with enough historical care to distinguish documented connections from claims that remain contested.
